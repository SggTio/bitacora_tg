1. The Lie Groupoids Paper
The paper is "A Diffeomorphism Groupoid and Algebroid Framework for Discontinuous Image Registration" arXiv, posted on arXiv as 2603.11806 (so it appeared in late March 2025, very recent). The authors propose a framework for piecewise diffeomorphic image registration involving discontinuous sliding motion, extending the diffeomorphism Lie groups to discontinuous diffeomorphism Lie groupoids. They allow discontinuities along sliding boundaries while maintaining diffeomorphism within homogeneous regions, and they derive specific Euler-Arnold equations for optimal flows in this context. arXiv
The motivating application is lung registration — during breathing, lung tissues slide discontinuously against surrounding structures, which violates the smoothness assumption in standard LDDMM. arXiv The mathematical content is substantial: they construct the Lie algebroid associated with the groupoid of discontinuous diffeomorphisms and derive the Euler-Poincaré equations in this setting. This is genuinely new — Euler-Poincaré reduction had only been done on Lie groups, not groupoids, in the registration context.
It's also worth noting a closely related 2025 paper by Mouhli and Pierron (arXiv 2511.14151): they extend LDDMM by enriching diffeomorphic deformations with transformations induced by finite-dimensional Lie groups (isometries, scalings) using semidirect products, endowed with a right-invariant sub-Riemannian structure that gives rise to new variational problems for shape registration. arXiv This is a different but complementary extension — decoupling global rigid/affine motions from local diffeomorphic deformations via reduction theory on semidirect products.
Both papers signal that the algebraic structure of the transformation group in LDDMM is actively being generalized, which opens mathematical research questions around the geometry of groupoids, algebroids, and semidirect products in infinite dimensions.

2. Riemannian Adam
The foundational paper is by Gary Bécigneul and Octavian-Eugen Ganea, "Riemannian Adaptive Optimization Methods" (ICLR 2019, arXiv 1810.00760).
To understand what they did and why it's nontrivial, let me walk through the conceptual chain.
Why Adam isn't trivially portable to manifolds. Recall that Euclidean Adam maintains two running statistics for each parameter: a first moment estimate (momentum) and a second moment estimate (adaptive learning rate). The second moment is element-wise — Adam rescales the gradient coordinate-by-coordinate, dividing by the square root of the running average of squared gradients. This is what makes it "adaptive": parameters that have received large gradients get smaller learning rates.
The problem on a general Riemannian manifold is that there are no canonical coordinates. On a manifold M, the gradient lives in the tangent space T_x M, and as x moves, the tangent space changes. You can't just accumulate squared gradients across iterations because they live in different vector spaces. You'd need to parallel transport previous moments to the current tangent space, but parallel transport depends on the path taken, and on curved manifolds, the transported vectors depend on the curvature — this introduces geometric complications that have no Euclidean analogue.
Bécigneul and Ganea's solution. They provide algorithms and convergence proofs for geodesically convex objectives in the particular case of a product of Riemannian manifolds, where adaptivity is implemented across manifolds in the Cartesian product. arXiv The key insight is to treat each factor manifold M_i in a product M = M_1 × M_2 × ... × M_N as a single "coordinate." Within each factor, they use a scalar adaptive learning rate (the norm of the Riemannian gradient on that factor), rather than trying to do element-wise adaptivity within the tangent space of each manifold. This sidesteps the coordinate problem elegantly: you don't need intrinsic coordinates on each M_i, only a way to compute norms and do Riemannian SGD within each factor.
Concretely, the Riemannian Adam update at each step does the following: (a) compute the Riemannian gradient (project the Euclidean gradient onto the tangent space), (b) update the first moment via parallel transport of the previous moment plus the new gradient, (c) update the second moment as a running average of squared gradient norms per factor, and (d) take a retraction step (an efficient approximation to the exponential map) in the direction of the moment-corrected, adaptively-scaled gradient.
Their generalization is tight — choosing Euclidean space as the Riemannian manifold recovers the standard algorithms and regret bounds exactly. arXiv Their initial motivation was learning embeddings of symbolic hierarchies (like WordNet) in hyperbolic space, where Riemannian SGD was already being used but adaptive methods were missing. OpenReview
The software ecosystem makes this immediately usable: the geoopt library provides RiemannianAdam as a subclass of torch.optim.Adam with the same API, supporting manifolds including Stiefel, SPD, Poincaré ball, and product manifolds. GitHub
Subsequent developments. A 2025 paper by Brantner et al. (arXiv 2305.16901) generalizes Adam to homogeneous spaces (like Stiefel manifolds) using the Lie group structure, lifting optimization to the group and using the Lie algebra as a global coordinate system arXiv — this allows full element-wise adaptivity rather than just per-factor adaptivity, at the cost of requiring homogeneous space structure.
For your purposes, the key takeaway is that Riemannian optimization is not just a theoretical curiosity — it's becoming standard infrastructure in machine learning. The mathematical questions that remain open involve convergence rates under various curvature assumptions, acceleration on manifolds (there's no clean Nesterov analogue yet), and understanding when the Riemannian geometry of the constraint set actually helps versus just being a convenient reformulation.

3. Building a PhD Direction from Your Master's Thesis
Your background — TDA-SegUNet and IConDiffNet for tumor segmentation — actually positions you at a very interesting intersection. Let me think through this carefully.
What you already have. IConDiffNet is a diffeomorphic registration network that incorporates inverse consistency and diffeomorphic properties through symmetric paths with recursive cascaded updating blocks that estimate time-dependent velocity fields PubMed. TDA-SegUNet combines topological data analysis with segmentation. So you already work at the junction of diffeomorphisms (Lie group/manifold theory) and persistent homology (algebraic topology) in medical imaging.
Does your proposed direction make sense? Yes, and I think it's quite natural, but let me sharpen what "deeper research" could mean compared to just "more experiments." The key question for a PhD in applied mathematics (as opposed to applied ML) is: what new mathematical theory are you developing?
Here are three concrete research directions that extend your thesis work toward genuine mathematical depth, ordered roughly by how close they are to what you already do:
Direction A: Topology-preserving diffeomorphic segmentation with Lie group constraints. This is closest to your current work. The idea: instead of using diffeomorphic registration as a black box and TDA as a post-hoc quality check, build a framework where the segmentation is defined as a diffeomorphic deformation of a template, and the topological constraints are enforced structurally through the group action. The TPSN paper by Lui's group at CUHK (arXiv 2202.12894) does exactly this: a template mask with the prescribed topology is deformed by a diffeomorphism to segment the object, and the diffeomorphic property is controlled by regularizing the Jacobian. DeepAI The Lui group also has a beautiful SIAM J. Imaging Sciences paper using quasiconformal Teichmüller theory to learn Beltrami coefficients as latent features in deep networks, where the diffeomorphic property is controlled via a simple activation function. SIAM
Your PhD contribution could be: what happens when the template has a Lie group symmetry (e.g., tumors that are approximately spherical, or tubular structures with SO(2) symmetry)? You could develop equivariant diffeomorphic segmentation where the deformation field respects the symmetry group of the template — this would reduce the dimensionality of the problem and provide structural guarantees. The mathematics would involve equivariant LDDMM (Euler-Poincaré reduction with isotropy subgroups), equivariant neural ODEs, and the interaction between the symmetry group and the topological constraints. This is a natural extension of both your IConDiffNet work (diffeomorphic registration) and your TDA work (topological constraints).
Direction B: Riemannian geometry of segmentation quality spaces. A more geometrically ambitious direction. The space of all segmentations of a given image with fixed topology is itself a manifold (it's a subspace of the diffeomorphism group acting on a template). The question: what is the right Riemannian metric on this space? Different metrics (LDDMM-type, elastic, Sobolev) give different notions of "nearby segmentations" and different geodesics between them. You could develop Riemannian statistics on segmentation spaces — Fréchet means of segmentations, principal geodesic analysis of segmentation variability across patients, geodesic regression of tumor evolution over time. This connects to the shape space community (Bauer, Pennec, Srivastava) and would produce new theory about metrics on infinite-dimensional spaces with topological constraints. The deliverables would be concrete: better uncertainty quantification for segmentation, statistical atlases of tumor shapes, and geodesic interpolation for longitudinal studies.
Direction C: Multi-parameter persistent homology for diffeomorphic image analysis. The most algebraically deep direction. Current TDA in segmentation uses single-parameter persistent homology (filtering by, say, probability threshold). But diffeomorphic registration gives you a second natural parameter: the "time" along the diffeomorphic flow. You could develop a two-parameter persistence theory where one parameter is the filtration level and the other is the deformation parameter. The mathematical challenge is that multi-parameter persistence has no canonical barcode — this connects to the frontier work by Oudot, Botnan, and collaborators on signed barcodes and rank decompositions. The payoff: you could detect topological changes that occur during the registration process (e.g., a tumor that breaks into components as you deform toward a template), which current methods miss entirely.
My honest recommendation. Direction A is the safest and most natural extension of your work — it has clear deliverables, connects to active communities (Lui's group, the LDDMM community, the equivariant deep learning community), and lets you contribute both theory and implementations. Direction B is richer mathematically but harder to scope for a single PhD. Direction C is the most novel but also the most speculative.
If I were advising you, I'd suggest starting with Direction A as the core of your PhD proposal, with elements of B (the Riemannian perspective on segmentation spaces) as the theoretical deepening that makes it a mathematics PhD rather than an engineering PhD. The key groups to look at are: Ronald Lui's group at CUHK (quasiconformal methods for diffeomorphic segmentation), Nicolas Charon at Johns Hopkins (LDDMM with shape constraints), and the topology-aware segmentation community around Chao Chen at Stony Brook (whose student Saumya Gupta just published TopoDiffusionNet at ICLR 2025, combining diffusion models with topological constraints).
The phrase you used — "under heavy restrictions of Lie group actions" — is exactly the right intuition. Restricting the diffeomorphism group to respect a symmetry group is mathematically called equivariant reduction, and it's a well-developed theory in geometric mechanics that has barely been applied to segmentation. That's a genuine gap you could fill.
